The checklist before the interview.

This site covers system design. But a senior backend interview is 4-5 rounds: system design, coding, behavioural, domain depth, and sometimes architecture review. This page maps every area, shows what's covered and what's not, and gives you a 4-week study plan.

Covered 1. System Design

The core of senior interviews. You're strong here — review, don't re-learn.
Review all 7 pillars — can you explain each concept from memory?
Pick 3 random concepts per pillar. Explain the diagram, the trade-off, and when you'd use it vs not. If you can't, re-read that section.

Practice the 35-minute system design walkthrough format
Requirements (5 min) → estimation (5 min) → high-level design (10 min) → deep-dive on 2-3 components (12 min) → bottlenecks + trade-offs (3 min). Practice with a timer.
Practice prompts: "Design a payment system" · "Design an order tracking service" · "Design a rate limiter"

Gap 2. Coding Rounds

The biggest gap. Senior coding rounds test practical problem-solving, not LeetCode hards. Focus on medium-level problems in your primary language.
Hash maps & sets — frequency counting, two-sum variants, grouping
The most common data structure in senior coding rounds. Practice: group anagrams, LRU cache, find duplicates, two-sum.
LeetCode: #1 Two Sum · #146 LRU Cache · #49 Group Anagrams
Trees & graphs — BFS, DFS, topological sort
Dependency resolution (topological sort), shortest path, tree traversal. Practice: course schedule, number of islands, serialize/deserialize tree.
LeetCode: #200 Number of Islands · #207 Course Schedule · #297 Serialize Tree
Sliding window & two pointers
Rate limiter implementation, stream processing windows, substring problems. Practice: longest substring without repeating, minimum window substring.
LeetCode: #3 Longest Substring · #76 Minimum Window · #239 Sliding Window Max
Concurrency — mutexes, channels, producer-consumer, thread safety
The senior differentiator. Implement a thread-safe queue, a connection pool, a rate limiter with token bucket. Know your language's concurrency model cold (goroutines / asyncio / virtual threads).
Go: "Concurrency in Go" (Cox) · Python: "Python Concurrency" (real-python.com) · Java: "Java Concurrency in Practice" (Goetz)
System-level coding — implement a real component
"Implement a key-value store with TTL", "implement a task scheduler", "implement a pub-sub system". These test whether you can translate system design into working code.
Practice: implement an in-memory cache with LRU eviction + TTL in 45 minutes
Practice cadence: 2-3 problems/day for 2-3 weeks
Don't grind 500 problems. Do 50-70 well-chosen mediums across the categories above. Focus on explaining your approach out loud as you code — that's what the interviewer evaluates.
NeetCode 150 (curated list) · Grind 75 (shorter version)

Gap 3. Behavioural & Leadership

Every company has 1-2 behavioural rounds. You can't cram these — you need to mine your own experience for stories. Prepare 6-8 stories that cover the themes below.
Prepare 6-8 STAR stories from your experience
Situation → Task → Action → Result. Each story should be 2-3 minutes when told aloud. Write them down, practice telling them to a friend or recording yourself.
Story: "Tell me about a time you drove a technical decision through ambiguity"
They want: how you gathered info, built consensus, made a call with incomplete data, and owned the outcome. Pick a real architecture decision you made.
Story: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate/manager"
They want: respectful pushback, data-driven argument, willingness to disagree-and-commit. Don't pick a story where you were "right" — pick one where the process was good.
Story: "Tell me about a time you mentored someone"
Senior = multiplier. How did you help a junior grow? Pair programming, code review, 1:1s, stretch assignments? What was the outcome for them?
Story: "Tell me about a production incident you handled"
Detection → triage → mitigation → postmortem. Focus on your role: did you lead, coordinate, debug? What did the system change afterwards? Ties directly to your page 05.
Incident response (page 05)
Story: "Tell me about a time you had to say no / push back on scope"
Senior engineers protect the team from overcommitment. How did you evaluate the trade-off, communicate it, and propose an alternative?
Story: "What's a project you're most proud of?"
Pick something with technical depth AND impact. Be ready to go deep on the architecture AND explain why it mattered to the business.

Partial 4. Back-of-Envelope Estimation

Comes up in the first 5 minutes of every system design round. You know the latency numbers (page 06) — now learn the framework.
Memorise the estimation framework
DAU → requests/day → peak QPS (×3-5 of average) → storage per record × retention → bandwidth → cache size (follow the 80/20 rule: cache 20% of daily reads).
Memorise key numbers
1 day = 86,400s ≈ 100k. 1 million requests/day ≈ 12 QPS. 1 billion/day ≈ 12k QPS. 1 char = 1 byte. 1 KB ≈ a short JSON. 1 MB ≈ a photo. 1 GB = 1 million short JSONs. 1 TB = 1 billion short JSONs.
Practice: estimate storage & QPS for 3 systems
"Design a URL shortener for 100M DAU" — how many URLs/day? Storage per URL? Total storage in 5 years? Peak write QPS? Read:write ratio? Cache size?
"System Design Interview" Vol 1 Ch 2 (Alex Xu) — best estimation walkthrough
Know when estimation matters and when it doesn't
Estimation for "do we need sharding?" (yes, if QPS > single-node capacity). Don't waste time estimating to 3 decimal places — order of magnitude is enough.
Latency numbers (page 06)

Partial 5. Networking & Infrastructure

Not usually a dedicated round, but comes up in system design deep-dives. "What happens when the user clicks submit?" is a classic probe.
TCP vs UDP — when and why
TCP: reliable, ordered, connection-oriented (HTTP, databases). UDP: fast, connectionless, no guarantee (video streaming, DNS, gaming). Know the handshake (SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK).
DNS resolution — the full chain
Browser cache → OS cache → recursive resolver → root → TLD → authoritative. TTL controls caching. Failover via multiple A records or CNAME + health checks (Route 53, Cloudflare).
HTTP/1.1 vs HTTP/2 vs HTTP/3
HTTP/1.1: one request per connection (head-of-line blocking). HTTP/2: multiplexed streams, header compression, server push. HTTP/3: QUIC (UDP-based), no TCP head-of-line blocking.
TLS handshake — what actually happens
ClientHello → ServerHello + cert → key exchange → encrypted session. TLS 1.3 is one round-trip (vs two for 1.2). Know how mTLS adds client cert verification.
mTLS (page 07)
Kubernetes concepts — pods, services, deployments, HPA
You don't need to write YAML, but explain: why pods (not containers) are the unit, how services do load balancing (kube-proxy, DNS), how HPA scales on CPU/custom metrics, liveness vs readiness probes.
Deployment strategies (page 05)
Load balancing — L4 vs L7, algorithms
L4 (TCP level, fast, no content inspection) vs L7 (HTTP level, can route by path/header). Algorithms: round-robin, least-connections, consistent hashing. When to use each.

Partial 6. SQL & Schema Design

Some companies have a dedicated "design the schema" round. Others probe it during system design. Your page 02 covers concepts; this is about practical skill.
Design a schema from requirements — normalised first, then denormalise
Start normalised (3NF), identify the hot read queries, denormalise only where needed. Know when to add a materialised view vs a separate read table (CQRS).
Data storage (page 02)
Index design — which columns, composite indexes, covering indexes
Rule: index the columns in your WHERE + ORDER BY + JOIN ON. Composite index order matters (leftmost prefix). Covering index avoids table lookup. EXPLAIN ANALYZE to verify.
Write complex queries — JOINs, window functions, CTEs
Practice: "find the top 3 products per category by revenue" (window function), "find users who ordered in Jan but not Feb" (LEFT JOIN + IS NULL or NOT EXISTS).
SQLZoo.net · PostgreSQL Exercises (pgexercises.com)
Migration strategies for large tables
Adding a column to a 100M-row table: nullable first, backfill in batches, add constraint last. Never ALTER TABLE ADD COLUMN NOT NULL DEFAULT on a huge table in Postgres without checking lock behaviour.
Zero-downtime migrations (page 05)

Four weeks to interview-ready

Assumes 2-3 hours/day. Adjust the pace to your timeline. The order matters — week 1 fills the biggest gap (coding), week 4 is polish.

W1
Coding foundations + behavioural stories
Morning (1.5h): 2-3 LeetCode mediums — hash maps, sliding window, trees. Explain your approach out loud.
Evening (1h): Write 6 STAR stories. Tell each one aloud in under 3 minutes. Record yourself if possible.
Weekend: Read DDIA chapters 5-7 (replication, partitioning, transactions).
W2
Coding depth + system design practice
Morning (1.5h): 2-3 coding problems — graphs (BFS/DFS), concurrency (implement a rate limiter, thread-safe cache).
Evening (1h): One full system design mock per day (35 min + 10 min self-review). Use the applied pages as templates.
Weekend: Read "Database Internals" chapters on B-trees and LSM trees. Practice estimation on 3 prompts.
W3
Domain depth + mock interviews
Morning (1h): Review payment system and logistics pages. Can you draw each diagram from memory?
Afternoon (1.5h): Do 2 mock interviews with a friend or on Pramp/interviewing.io — one system design, one coding.
Evening (30m): Review networking concepts (TCP, DNS, TLS, HTTP/2, K8s). Read the Stripe and Uber blog posts referenced in the applied pages.
Weekend: Read "A Philosophy of Software Design" (short book, can finish in a weekend).
W4
Polish + company-specific prep
Morning (1h): Light coding — review your weak patterns, re-do 5 problems you struggled with.
Afternoon (1h): Research the company: their tech blog, their stack, their domain. Prepare 2-3 questions for each interviewer.
Evening (30m): Review STAR stories. Practice "tell me about yourself" (2 min pitch: background → current role → why this company).
Day before: Light review only. Get sleep. The prep is done.
Interview day reminders
System design
Drive the conversation. Clarify requirements before drawing. State trade-offs explicitly — "I'm choosing X over Y because..." The interviewer wants to see how you think, not a perfect answer.
Coding
Talk through your approach before coding. Start with the brute force, then optimise. Test with an example. Handle edge cases. Naming matters — reserveInventory() not doStuff().
Behavioural
Be specific, not generic. "I reduced P99 latency by 40% by adding a cache layer" beats "I improved performance." Use numbers when you have them.
General
Ask good questions at the end — about the team's biggest challenge, their deployment cadence, how they handle tech debt. These show you're evaluating them too.
