Architecture practice · Decisions

Write down why, not just what.

Code shows what a system does. It never says why it is built that way, which options lost, or what would change the answer. An architecture decision record (ADR) is a one-page note that does. This page covers what counts as an architecture decision, what goes in the record, how records live and change, how to write one people trust, and how to use them in reviews and interviews. The examples follow Northwind Cold Chain's data platform.

Concepts6
Reading time30 min
FormatsMarkdown · Shell · Policy
PRINCIPLE 01

Decisions decay; records don't.

The reasons behind a choice leave with the people who made it. A one-page record outlives them, and tells the next person whether the reasons still hold.

PRINCIPLE 02

Significant means hard to reverse.

Record the one-way doors: platforms, protocols, identity, regions, and the things you chose not to do. Leave library choices to the pull request.

PRINCIPLE 03

The options are the point.

A decision without the alternatives it beat is an announcement. Describe each option as its supporters would, then say what your choice costs.

PRINCIPLE 04

Supersede, never edit.

An accepted record is history. When the answer changes, write a new one that links back, and name the triggers that would change it again.

01

What an ADR is · the why that code cannot hold

Start herePractice
Difficulty

The story

A year after the lakehouse goes live, a new architect asks why the finance marts run on DuckDB and dbt when the whole company runs on Microsoft 365 and Fabric is one licence away. The people who decided have moved on. Without a record, the team has two bad options: argue it again from nothing, or keep it out of superstition (“they must have had a reason”). An architecture decision record (ADR) is the fix: a short text file, one per decision, that says what was decided, in what situation, which options lost, and what the decision costs. Michael Nygard proposed the format in 2011 with five parts: title, status, context, decision, consequences. Every richer template since is a variation on that.

An ADR is not a design document. A design document describes how a whole system works and changes as the system changes; an ADR captures one decision at one point in time, with the forces acting on it then, and is never rewritten afterwards. Together, the numbered ADRs form a decision log: the architecture's memory. They live in the repository next to the code they govern, are reviewed as pull requests, and are read by the next person who wonders why. Code tells you what the system does; it can never tell you which alternatives were weighed, or what would have to change for the answer to change.

Trade-off: writing time vs memory. A good ADR takes 20 to 60 minutes. It pays that back the first time someone asks why, at every onboarding, in every audit, and above all when circumstances change: a record that names its assumptions tells you whether the decision still holds or whether its time is up. The cost of not writing one is invisible until it is large.

Use when
  • A decision will outlive the people who made it
  • Teams rotate, or vendors and contractors come and go
  • Regulated or audited environments where “why” must be shown
Avoid
  • ADRs as an approval form filled in before any thinking
  • Writing them after the fact to justify a choice already made
  • One giant document holding every decision

02

When to write one · architecturally significant decisions

PracticeCore
Difficulty

The story

Not every choice deserves a record; a log of trivia hides the decisions that matter. Write an ADR for an architecturally significant decision. Four practical tests: it is hard or costly to reverse (a one-way door: a data platform, an identity provider, a region); it crosses a boundary, so other teams or systems must live with it; it changes a quality attribute such as security, cost, latency, availability or operability; or there are real options on which reasonable people disagree. Any yes, write one. All no, and the pull request description or a code comment is enough: which JSON library a service uses, naming, formatting.

Two kinds get forgotten. Decisions not to do something (not adopting Kubernetes, not splitting the monolith yet, not buying a feature store) are the ones re-asked most often, so they deserve a record most. And constraints you accept from outside (the company standard is Entra ID; POPIA requires personal data in South Africa) are decisions for your system even when someone else made them: record that you accepted them and what they cost. A small team typically writes ten to twenty ADRs a year; a hundred means the bar is too low, none means decisions are happening in meetings and being lost.

Trade-off: completeness vs signal. Too few and the reasoning is lost; too many and nobody reads them. Use the test above, and when in doubt, write a short one: three paragraphs that exist beat a perfect template that does not.

Use when
  • Choosing a platform, protocol, identity model or region
  • Saying no to something that will be proposed again
  • Accepting a constraint that shapes your design
Avoid
  • ADRs for library choices inside one service
  • A decision log nobody reads because it is 90% trivia
  • Deciding in meetings and recording nothing

# 11. Do not adopt Kubernetes for the data platform

Status: Accepted · 2025-06-02

## Context

The platform runs four containers (Dagster, dbt runner, DuckDB service, an
ingest API). Two engineers operate it. The lake is object storage; nothing
needs autoscaling beyond one VM's headroom for at least 12 months.

## Decision

We will run the containers on Azure Container Apps, not AKS or any other
Kubernetes cluster.

## Consequences

- Less control over scheduling and networking; one region only.
- Revisit if we exceed 20 containers or need a capability Container Apps lacks.
03

What goes in one · the template, field by field

PracticeCore
Difficulty

The story

A useful ADR has nine parts. The title is the decision as a short imperative phrase (Run the finance marts on DuckDB and dbt), with a number that is never reused. Status and date: proposed, accepted, rejected, deprecated or superseded. Context is the situation and its forces: requirements, constraints, quality attributes, with numbers (300 GB, two engineers, ready by 06:00), written so neutrally that the people who preferred the losing option would agree with it. Decision drivers are the qualities that matter most, ranked. Considered options are at least two real ones, plus “do nothing” when that is genuinely possible, each with its pros and cons.

The decision reads “we will … because …”, and the because points back at the drivers. Consequences list what becomes easier and what becomes harder, including costs, risks and their mitigations; an ADR whose consequences are all good is a sales pitch. Confirmation says how you will know it was implemented and works: a test, a policy, a metric. Revisit when names the triggers that would reopen it (data over 2 TB, a 15-minute SLO, a third engineer), which turns a decision into something you can check. Two well-known formats: Nygard's minimal five sections, and MADR (Markdown Any Decision Records), which adds drivers, options and confirmation. A one-sentence Y-statement makes a good summary line: in the context of …, facing …, we decided for … and against …, to achieve …, accepting ….

Trade-off: minimal vs complete. Nygard's template is quick and gets written; MADR's is thorough and sometimes does not. Start minimal and add options and triggers, the two parts that most improve an ADR. Length is not quality: one to two pages, and link to longer analysis instead of pasting it.

Use when
  • Any architecturally significant decision
  • Choices with real alternatives that will be questioned later
  • Decisions with conditions under which they stop being right
Avoid
  • Context written as an argument for the winner
  • Options listed without their downsides
  • Consequences that are all good
  • No revisit triggers, so nobody knows when it expires

04

Lifecycle and home · numbered, immutable, superseded

PracticeOperations
Difficulty

The story

ADRs live in the repository next to the code they govern, as numbered Markdown files in docs/adr/; cross-cutting decisions (identity, landing zone, network) go in an architecture repository that service repos link to. The first ADR is the decision to keep ADRs. A new ADR starts as Proposed in a pull request: the PR discussion is the decision meeting, and the approvals are the deciders' sign-off. Merged, it is Accepted; declined, it is kept as Rejected with the reason, because the next person to propose it should find that answer.

Accepted ADRs are immutable. When the situation changes, you do not edit ADR-0007; you write ADR-0012, Move the finance marts to Fabric, which says “supersedes ADR-0007” and links back, and ADR-0007's status changes to Superseded by 0012. Only status and links ever change on an old record. That history is the value: it shows what was believed when, and why the answer changed, which is exactly what an auditor or a new architect needs. Decisions that stop applying without a replacement, say when a system is retired, become Deprecated. Keep an index (a README generated by adr-tools or log4brains) so the log can be read as a list.

Trade-off: repository vs wiki. In the repository, ADRs are versioned, reviewed like code and seen by the engineers who need them; in a wiki, managers and other teams find them more easily but they drift from the code. Keep the repository as the source and publish a rendered view (a static site, or a wiki page generated from it) for everyone else.

Use when
  • Every team that writes code against an architecture
  • Decisions that need a visible approval trail
  • Platforms where several repos share one set of decisions
Avoid
  • Editing an accepted ADR to match a new decision
  • Deleting rejected ADRs
  • ADRs in a wiki nobody updates while the code moves on

# scripts/check_adrs.py: run in CI. Plain Python, no service: it reads files.
# Fails when an ADR has no valid status, a required section is missing, or a
# "Superseded by" points at an ADR that does not exist.
import re
import sys
from pathlib import Path

REQUIRED = ["## Context", "## Decision", "## Consequences"]
STATUSES = ("Proposed", "Accepted", "Rejected", "Deprecated", "Superseded by")

adrs = sorted(Path("docs/adr").glob("[0-9][0-9][0-9][0-9]-*.md"))
numbers = {int(p.name[:4]) for p in adrs}
problems = []

for path in adrs:
    text = path.read_text(encoding="utf-8")
    status = re.search(r"Status:\**\s*(.+)", text) or re.search(r"## Status\s+(.+)", text)
    if not status or not status.group(1).strip().startswith(STATUSES):
        problems.append(f"{path.name}: no valid status")
    for heading in REQUIRED:
        if heading not in text:
            problems.append(f"{path.name}: missing {heading}")
    for ref in re.findall(r"Superseded by \[?(\d{4})", text):
        if int(ref) not in numbers:
            problems.append(f"{path.name}: superseded by {ref}, which does not exist")

print("\n".join(problems) or f"{len(adrs)} ADRs OK")
sys.exit(1 if problems else 0)
05

Writing one people trust · a worked example

PracticeDeep dive
Difficulty

The story

A trustworthy ADR reads like an honest briefing, not a sales pitch. Northwind's ADR-0007 starts from the facts: four entities, about 300 GB growing 20% a year, a month-end pack that must tie to each trial balance by 06:00, two data engineers, a Microsoft 365 estate with Power BI. It names the drivers in order: tie-out correctness, ready by 06:00, running cost, operating load, Power BI fit, cost to leave. It weighs three real options: (A) self-run DuckDB, dbt and Dagster on one VM; (B) Microsoft Fabric on an F-capacity; (C) Databricks. Each option is written up the way its supporter would describe it, then marked against the drivers.

The decision is A, because it meets the two hard drivers at the lowest running cost and the data fits one machine for at least two years. The consequences include the ones that hurt: the two engineers operate and upgrade it, DuckDB allows one writer at a time, and Power BI imports rather than reading live. Each has a mitigation or an owner. Then the triggers: data above 2 TB, a freshness SLO under 15 minutes, or a third engineer joining. Eighteen months later the freshness trigger fires, and ADR-0012 proposes Fabric, supersedes 0007, and is approved in a week, because nobody has to reconstruct the original reasoning. That is what the record is for.

What makes an ADR trusted: numbers instead of adjectives (“300 GB” rather than “small”); steelmanned alternatives, so the losing side recognises its own argument; consequences that hurt; measurable triggers; and brevity, linking to the spike results or cost model rather than pasting them. Trade-off: rigour vs speed. Time-box the analysis: run a short spike, write the ADR from what you learned, and let the triggers cover what you could not know yet.

Use when
  • Platform and vendor choices with a multi-year cost
  • Decisions you will present to a review board or in an interview
  • Any decision whose right answer depends on growth
Avoid
  • Adjectives in place of measurements
  • A straw-man version of the option you rejected
  • Hiding the downside in the hope nobody asks
  • Pasting the whole cost model into the ADR

# 7. Run the finance marts on DuckDB and dbt

- **Status:** Superseded by [0012](0012-move-finance-marts-to-fabric.md)
- **Date:** 2025-03-14
- **Deciders:** Head of data, lead data engineer   **Consulted:** Finance, security

## Context

Four entities, about 300 GB, growing roughly 20% a year. The month-end pack
must tie to each entity's trial balance and be ready by 06:00. Two data
engineers run the platform. The company runs Microsoft 365 and Power BI.

## Decision drivers

1. Totals tie to the source exactly
2. Ready by 06:00 on 99% of days
3. Running cost
4. Operating load on two people
5. Power BI fit
6. Cost to leave

## Considered options

1. **Self-run:** DuckDB, dbt, Dagster on one VM; Parquet on Blob Storage.
2. **Fabric:** OneLake, Delta, Direct Lake to Power BI, on an F-capacity.
3. **Databricks:** Unity Catalog, Delta, SQL warehouse.

| Driver           | Self-run | Fabric      | Databricks |
|------------------|----------|-------------|------------|
| Running cost     | low      | medium      | high       |
| Ops load         | high     | low         | medium     |
| Ready by 06:00   | yes      | yes         | yes        |
| Power BI fit     | import   | Direct Lake | connector  |
| Cost to leave    | low      | medium      | medium     |

## Decision

We will run the finance marts on DuckDB and dbt (option 1), because it meets
drivers 1 and 2 at the lowest running cost, and the data fits one machine
for at least two years.

## Consequences

- Good: lowest cost; everything runs on a laptop; open formats keep the exit cheap.
- Bad: we operate and upgrade it (mitigation: a runbook, monthly patch window).
- Bad: DuckDB allows one writer (mitigation: Dagster serialises the writes).
- Bad: Power BI imports on a schedule; no live reads.

## Confirmation

Reconciliation blocks any publish that does not tie; a freshness SLO alert
fires if gold is not ready by 06:00.

## Revisit when

- Data exceeds 2 TB
- Finance needs a freshness SLO under 15 minutes
- The team grows past two engineers
# 12. Move the finance marts to Fabric

- **Status:** Accepted
- **Date:** 2026-09-08
- **Supersedes:** [0007](0007-run-finance-marts-on-duckdb-and-dbt.md)

## Context

ADR-0007's second trigger has fired: operations now need stock and margin
within 15 minutes of a sale, and a third engineer has joined. Data is 700 GB.

06

Reviews, governance and interviews · ADRs in use

PracticeArchitecture
Difficulty

The story

ADRs change how architecture is governed. A review board that meets weekly to approve slides becomes a bottleneck; one that reads Proposed ADRs asynchronously, and only above a line (spend over a threshold, a security boundary, a change that affects other teams), keeps decisions moving. Below the line, the advice process works well: anyone may make a decision after seeking advice from the people it affects and from those with relevant expertise, and the ADR records that advice. Accepted decisions should be enforced, not remembered: a fitness function in CI, or a policy such as Azure Policy denying storage accounts with public network access, with the ADR number in its description so the rule explains itself.

For a solutions architect, the decision log is also the portfolio. AZ-305 case studies are ADR prompts in disguise: requirements and constraints in, a justified choice out. In a design interview, narrate every major choice in ADR shape: the context and its numbers, two or three options, the decision and why, the consequences you accept, and what would change your mind. In a behavioural round, “tell me about a difficult technical decision” is answered best from a real ADR: the forces, the alternatives, the outcome, and what happened when a trigger fired. Keep two or three sanitised examples ready.

Trade-off: governance vs autonomy. Central approval of everything is slow and teaches teams to avoid writing ADRs; no review at all lets decisions conflict across teams. Set the line explicitly, publish it, review above it asynchronously, and let teams own everything below it.

Use when
  • Several teams share a platform or landing zone
  • Decisions carry security or cost risk above a threshold
  • Preparing for AZ-305 case studies and design interviews
Avoid
  • A review board that approves slides, not records
  • Decisions enforced only by memory
  • Interview answers that name a tool without the trade-off
