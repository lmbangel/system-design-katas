Architecture practice · Governance

One rule, every door.

Northwind Cold Chain, the fictional group from the lakehouse and AI assistant designs, had a data platform that worked and a governance story that did not: four ways into the same data, four different permission lists, personal information nobody had counted, and no way to show an auditor who saw what. This case study follows how the platform was brought under control, decision by decision: classify before you protect, keep one entitlement table and enforce it at every door, build POPIA into the pipeline, give every dataset an owner, and prove it continuously instead of promising it once a year. The company is fictional; the decisions are the ones every real data platform faces.

PRINCIPLE 01

Classify before you protect.

You cannot protect what you have not counted. Label every column first; policies attach to labels, not to tables or people.

PRINCIPLE 02

One rule, every door.

BI, SQL, APIs and the AI assistant read the same entitlement table, as the user. A second permission list is a leak waiting for a date.

PRINCIPLE 03

Don't keep what you don't need.

The safest personal information is the column you never loaded. Minimise at ingestion, mask in the middle, delete on a timer.

PRINCIPLE 04

Prove it, continuously.

Governance an auditor has to take on trust is not governance. Log every read, test every door daily, and keep the evidence ready.

01

The situation · four doors, four rulebooks

Start hereCase study
Difficulty

The story

Northwind's data platform had done its first job well. Three entities' ERPs and warehouse systems landed in one lakehouse, and finance trusted the reconciled marts. Then the questions changed. A Namibian dispatcher opened a Power BI report and saw South African debtors. An analyst's query on the SQL endpoint returned customer ID numbers, because nobody had decided they should not be there. The new AI assistant ran on a service account that could read everything. And every month, CSV exports of customer lists travelled by email with no owner and no expiry.

None of these was a breach yet, but each was the same failure: four doors into the same data, each with its own rulebook. Power BI had role lists per report, the SQL endpoint trusted analysts, the assistant trusted itself, and exports trusted whoever pressed the button. Rules kept in four places drift apart, and the gaps between them are where leaks happen. On top of that sat POPIA, South Africa's Protection of Personal Information Act, which requires a lawful purpose for personal information, reasonable security safeguards, limits on how long it is kept, and notifying the Information Regulator and the people affected after a security compromise.

The brief to the architect was short: one set of rules, enforced at every door, provable to an auditor, without stopping the business from using its data. The rest of this page is how that was done, in the order it was done. Order matters: you cannot write access rules for data you have not classified, and you cannot prove rules you do not log.

Ask first
  • How many ways in are there, including exports and the AI?
  • Where does personal information live, column by column?
  • Who decides who may see a dataset today, if anyone?
  • What would you show the Information Regulator tomorrow?
Red flags
  • Permission lists per tool, maintained by hand
  • Service accounts that read everything "for convenience"
  • Nobody can name the owner of a dataset
  • Governance as a policy document nobody can test

# Who can read what today? Compare each door's effective access to what it should be.
from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Door:
    name: str
    principals: set[str]          # users or service accounts with access
    entities_visible: set[str]    # entities those principals can read


def over_exposed(door: Door, entitled: dict[str, set[str]]) -> list[str]:
    """Principals who can see an entity they are not entitled to."""
    problems = []
    for p in sorted(door.principals):
        extra = door.entities_visible - entitled.get(p, set())
        if extra:
            problems.append(f"{door.name}: {p} can see {sorted(extra)}")
    return problems


# The baseline that made the case for the project: every door over-exposed someone.
02

Classify before you protect · labels, not lists

CoreGovernance
Difficulty

The story

The first decision was not about access at all. It was to count and label. Every column in silver and gold got one label from a short list: public, internal, confidential, personal, personal identifier and special personal. The last follows POPIA's special personal information category, which includes things such as health, religious beliefs and biometric data and carries stricter conditions. Five or six labels is enough; a scheme with twenty is never applied consistently.

Labelling 1 400 columns by hand is how these projects die, so a scanner proposed labels from column names and from the values themselves: a 13-digit string whose check digit passes the Luhn test is very likely a South African ID number, and an @ with a domain is an email address. The scanner only suggests. Each dataset's owner confirmed or corrected the labels, because a label is a decision someone must be accountable for.

Then the important design choice: policies attach to labels, not to tables or people. "Personal data is visible only to users entitled to that entity" is one rule, and it covers every personal column that exists now or later. "Personal identifiers are tokenised in silver" is another. "Special personal information is not loaded" is the strictest and the simplest. And the default protects the gaps: a new column arrives unlabelled and hidden until its owner labels it, so a schema change in the ERP cannot quietly expose something new.

Do
  • A short label list everyone can apply
  • Scanners that suggest; owners who decide
  • Policies written against labels
  • Unlabelled means hidden, by default
Avoid
  • Classifying tables instead of columns
  • Twenty labels nobody remembers
  • Trusting the scanner without an owner's review
  • New columns visible until someone notices

-- The catalog of labels: one row per column, set and confirmed by the owner.
create table governance.column_label (
    table_schema  text not null,
    table_name    text not null,
    column_name   text not null,
    label         text not null check (label in (
                      'public', 'internal', 'confidential',
                      'personal', 'personal_identifier', 'special_personal')),
    suggested_by  text not null,          -- 'scanner' or a person
    confirmed_by  text,                   -- the dataset owner; null = not yet
    confirmed_at  timestamptz,
    primary key (table_schema, table_name, column_name)
);

-- Unlabelled or unconfirmed columns: hidden from every door until this is empty.
select c.table_schema, c.table_name, c.column_name
from information_schema.columns c
left join governance.column_label l
  on l.table_schema = c.table_schema
 and l.table_name = c.table_name
 and l.column_name = c.column_name
where c.table_schema = 'gold'
  and (l.label is null or l.confirmed_by is null);
# Suggest labels from names and values. The owner confirms; the scanner never decides.
import re

EMAIL = re.compile(r"^[^@\s]+@[^@\s]+\.[a-z]{2,}$", re.I)
SA_ID = re.compile(r"^\d{13}$")


def luhn_ok(digits: str) -> bool:
    total = 0
    for i, ch in enumerate(reversed(digits)):
        n = int(ch)
        if i % 2 == 1:
            n = n * 2 - 9 if n > 4 else n * 2
        total += n
    return total % 10 == 0


def suggest(column: str, sample: list[str]) -> str | None:
    name = column.lower()
    values = [v for v in sample if v]
    if values and sum(bool(SA_ID.match(v) and luhn_ok(v)) for v in values) / len(values) > 0.8:
        return "personal_identifier"          # very likely SA ID numbers
    if values and sum(bool(EMAIL.match(v)) for v in values) / len(values) > 0.8:
        return "personal"
    if any(k in name for k in ("medical", "health", "religion", "biometric")):
        return "special_personal"
    if any(k in name for k in ("name", "phone", "address", "birth")):
        return "personal"
    return None                               # no suggestion; the owner labels it
# label-policies.yaml: rules attach to labels, so new columns inherit them.
labels:
  public: { visible_to: everyone }
  internal: { visible_to: all_staff }
  confidential: { visible_to: entitled_users }
  personal:
    visible_to: entitled_users          # entity-level entitlement (deep-dive 3)
    exports: deny
  personal_identifier:
    stored_as: token                    # raw value only in the bronze vault
    visible_to: nobody_by_default
  special_personal:
    load: false                         # not needed for any approved purpose
unlabelled:
  visible_to: nobody                    # until the owner labels it
03

One entitlement table · enforced at every door

CriticalCore
Difficulty

The story

With columns labelled, the question became which rows each person may see. The answer was one table, security.user_entity: a row per user and entity, with their cost centres and a clearance level. It is not edited by hand. A sync job fills it from Entra ID group memberships, so joiners, movers and leavers are handled by the process the business already runs. Disable someone's account and their access disappears from every door at the next sync.

Then each door was changed to read that table instead of keeping its own list. Power BI uses dynamic row-level security: one role whose filter looks up the signed-in user in the table, instead of a role list per report. The SQL endpoint applies a row filter based on the session user. The Marts API checks scopes and the user's entities on every call. The AI assistant acts on behalf of the user and filters retrieval with the same table, as designed in Applied D. Column labels from deep-dive 2 decide what each permitted row shows.

The rule that made it hold: no door may have its own permission list, and no service account may read business data. Pipelines still need identities to write the marts, but those are workload identities that can write gold and read nothing a person would query through them. The result is the property the brief asked for: the same person sees the same rows whichever door they use, and access changes in one place.

Design rules
  • Entitlements derived from directory groups
  • Every door reads the same table, as the user
  • Dynamic RLS instead of role lists per report
  • Workload identities write; people read
Avoid
  • A permission list inside each tool
  • Hand-edited entitlement rows
  • A shared "reporting" account behind BI
  • Filtering in the UI instead of the query

# Fill the entitlement table from directory groups. Idempotent: safe to rerun.
# Group naming convention: data-<entity>-<clearance>, e.g. data-NCC-NA-2
import re
from datetime import datetime, timezone

GROUP = re.compile(r"^data-(NCC-[A-Z]{2})-(\d)$")


def desired_rows(memberships: dict[str, list[str]]) -> set[tuple[str, str, int]]:
    """memberships: user_oid -> group names, read from the directory."""
    rows = set()
    for user, groups in memberships.items():
        for g in groups:
            m = GROUP.match(g)
            if m:
                rows.add((user, m.group(1), int(m.group(2))))
    return rows


def sync(db, memberships: dict[str, list[str]]) -> dict[str, int]:
    want = desired_rows(memberships)
    have = db.current_rows()                     # same tuple shape
    now = datetime.now(timezone.utc)
    db.delete_rows(have - want)                  # leavers and movers lose access
    db.upsert_rows(want - have, synced_at=now)   # joiners gain it
    return {"added": len(want - have), "removed": len(have - want)}
# enforcement.yaml: every door, and how it reads the one table.
entitlement_source: security.user_entity

sync_every_minutes: 60

  sql_endpoint: { method: row_filter, session_setting: app.user_oid }
  marts_api: { method: scopes_and_entity_claim }
  ai_assistant: { method: on_behalf_of, retrieval_filter: true }
forbidden:
  - per_tool_permission_lists
  - service_accounts_reading_business_data
04

POPIA in the pipeline · minimise, mask, expire

CompliancePrivacy
Difficulty

The story

POPIA turned out to be easier to satisfy in the pipeline than in a policy binder. Its conditions for lawful processing include processing limitation (only what is necessary for the purpose), purpose specification, retention limits and security safeguards. Each became an engineering decision at a specific layer of the medallion architecture.

Minimise at the source: the extraction stopped pulling columns no approved purpose needed. The staff medical notes that had been landing "just in case" stopped landing; the safest personal information is the column you never load. Restrict bronze: raw data is a locked vault that pipelines read and people do not, with a 90-day retention timer. Protect in silver: ID numbers are replaced by stable tokens, so joins still work but the raw value never reaches gold. Expose least in gold: personal columns are masked unless the viewer is entitled to that entity, using the labels from deep-dive 2.

Around the pipeline, three things made it auditable. A purpose register lists each personal field, why it is processed and how long it is kept. A nightly retention job enforces the timers, so retention is a fact, not an intention. And the platform keeps data in region; any transfer outside South Africa is assessed against POPIA's cross-border rules first. When something does go wrong, POPIA requires notifying the Information Regulator and the people affected; the access log from deep-dive 6 is what makes it possible to say exactly who those people are.

Do
  • A purpose for every personal field, written down
  • Tokenise identifiers before they reach gold
  • Retention enforced by a job, per layer
  • The Information Officer reviews the register
Avoid
  • Landing personal data "just in case"
  • Raw ID numbers in analytics tables
  • Retention as a policy nobody runs
  • Masking in the report instead of the data

# Nightly: enforce retention per layer, and record what was removed.
from datetime import date, timedelta

RETENTION_DAYS = {"bronze": 90, "silver.customer_inactive": 5 * 365}


def run(db, today: date) -> list[dict]:
    removed = []
    for target, days in RETENTION_DAYS.items():
        cutoff = today - timedelta(days=days)
        n = db.delete_older_than(target, cutoff)     # partition drop where possible
        removed.append({"target": target, "cutoff": cutoff.isoformat(), "rows": n})
    db.log("retention", removed)                     # evidence for deep-dive 6
    return removed

05

Ownership, contracts and access requests

OpsGovernance
Difficulty

The story

Rules need people who own them. Every domain (customers, orders, inventory, finance, people) got a named owner, a business head who decides who may see the data and why, and a steward who keeps its labels and contract current. The platform team's role was redrawn just as clearly: it enforces access decisions and never makes them. That one sentence ended the old habit of engineers granting access to whoever asked politely.

Access became a short, visible process. A person requests access with a reason and a duration; the dataset owner approves; approval adds a group membership; the sync from deep-dive 3 carries it to every door. Nothing is granted forever. Every quarter, owners receive the list of who has access to their data and re-approve or let it lapse. Access that nobody vouches for disappears, which is how permissions stop accumulating.

Each gold mart also got a data contract: its schema, grain, freshness promise, column labels and owner, kept next to the dbt model. A pipeline check compares any change against the contract. Adding a column is fine (it arrives unlabelled and hidden), but removing one, changing a type or changing the grain is a breaking change that needs the owner's approval before it can merge. Consumers, including the AI assistant, can rely on the contract instead of on hope.

Do
  • A named owner per domain who decides access
  • Requests with a reason and an expiry
  • Quarterly recertification by owners
  • Contracts checked in CI on every change
Avoid
  • Engineers granting access on request
  • Access that never expires
  • Owners in name only, who never review
  • Breaking a mart's shape without telling consumers

# CI: compare a model's new schema with its contract and flag breaking changes.
def breaking_changes(contract: dict, new_columns: dict[str, str], new_grain: str) -> list[str]:
    problems = []
    old = {c["name"]: c["type"] for c in contract["columns"]}
    for name, typ in old.items():
        if name not in new_columns:
            problems.append(f"removed column {name}")
        elif new_columns[name] != typ:
            problems.append(f"{name}: type {typ} -> {new_columns[name]}")
    if new_grain != contract["grain"]:
        problems.append(f"grain changed: {contract['grain']!r} -> {new_grain!r}")
    return problems   # non-empty: the merge needs the owner's approval


# New columns are not breaking. They arrive unlabelled and hidden until labelled.
-- Quarterly: what each owner must re-approve, and what lapses if they don't.
select g.owner,
       g.dataset,
       a.user_oid,
       a.granted_at,
       a.expires_at,
       a.reason
from governance.access_grant a
join governance.dataset g on g.dataset = a.dataset
where a.expires_at < now() + interval '30 days'
   or a.last_reviewed_at < now() - interval '90 days'
order by g.owner, g.dataset, a.expires_at;
06

Proving it · audit, leak tests and the evidence pack

CriticalEvidence
Difficulty

The story

The last step turned promises into evidence. Every door writes to one access log: who read, through which door, which dataset, which column labels and how many rows. With the AI assistant that includes the tool calls and retrieved documents, tied to the person who asked. The log is what answers the hard questions in minutes: who saw this customer's personal data this year, and, after an incident, exactly whose data was exposed, which POPIA's notification duty depends on.

Then the rules are tested the way code is. Every night, leak tests sign in as test users with known entitlements and try every door: a Namibia-only user queries South African rows through Power BI's dataset, the SQL endpoint, the API and the assistant. Any row that comes back fails the run and pages the platform team. The same suite runs in CI before any change to a view, a policy or the sync. Governance that is tested daily cannot drift silently.

Finally, a monthly evidence pack is generated, not assembled: the leak-test history, access-review outcomes, label coverage, the purpose register and retention-job results. The Information Officer and auditors read the same pack. The measurable results for Northwind: one source of access rules instead of four; every gold column labelled; zero cross-entity rows in daily tests; access requests granted in a day instead of a week; and the next audit took an afternoon. Each decision on this page has an ADR, so the next architect knows why.

Prove with
  • One access log across every door, including the AI
  • Daily leak tests as test users, per door
  • The same tests in CI before policy changes
  • An evidence pack generated, not assembled
Avoid
  • Logs per tool that cannot be joined
  • Testing access once, at go-live
  • Evidence assembled by hand before an audit
  • Metrics nobody owns or reads
